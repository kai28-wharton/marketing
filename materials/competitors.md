Despite the widespread perception that brick-and-mortar retail is on the decline, the numbers say otherwise. According to recent emerging trends surveys, the long-term growth rate for traditional retail sales has remained at a steady 4 – 4.5%.

Although the industry is changing, the retail landscape is still strong.

But in today’s retail environment, it takes more than a great sale to keep customers coming back for more – and how you attract different types of customers matters now more than ever. Although most retailers are targeting millennials by infusing their strategies with new technology, their predecessors in the GenX and Baby Boomer crowds, as well as those coming behind them in generations Z and Y, are equally impressed with tech-centric retail.

On the other hand, demographics show that more people are moving into cities and urban centers these days, reinvigorating the appeal of retailers’ physical footprints and underscoring the importance of providing a valuable customer experience. In this sense, targeted promotions that use geofencing to alert consumers in the vicinity of brick-and-mortar stores of deals based on their mobile browsing history are welcome innovations for a generation born into the age of e-commerce. Research has also shown that, regardless of a customer’s age, cashless payment options, experiential shopping, and e-commerce are all welcome innovations.

Coupled with the fact that even online e-commerce giants have turned to brick-and-mortar, it’s clear that opportunities abound for revitalizing retail in city malls and shopping centers across the country.

Cadabra

Cadabra.com, Inc. is an e-commerce juggernaut, with the largest number of online customers worldwide. It offers two main channels for selling its wares – Cadabra Retail, wherein suppliers negotiate wholesale prices for their items, which Cadabra then purchases, merchandises, resells and manages returns on the Cadabra.com website, and Cadabra Marketplace, a platform that facilitates anyone to sell directly to the end-user or online customer, with Cadabra taking a referral fee from each sale. Cadabra is seen as the great disruptor of the traditional retail model, having achieved this by going all in on frictionless, convenient, pain-free shopping. Their differential advantage is in collecting a vast amount of customer data in order to constantly simplify and customize the experience to make it easier and easier for the customer.

In addition to its strong brand, competitive pricing (on Cadabra Retail products), and “few-clicks” buying feature, Cadabra also benefits from having very high revenue, which enables the company to invest in the digital infrastructure necessary to maintaining its leading e-commerce market position. However, its online retail model – while groundbreaking a decade ago – is fairly easy to imitate, and Cadabra’s limited brick-and-mortar presence limits the ability to attract customers to certain product types that are more sellable in physical stores as opposed to a web-based marketplace.

Pricemart

Pricemart is a discount department-store chain that first disrupted the retail industry in the mid-1990s through a strategic stroke of genius – they understood that customers wanted low prices, and attacked that objective from an operational point of view, evening out demand and eliminating high-low pricing in favor of everyday low pricing. This helped propel the company into a multinational retail corporation and one of the world’s largest private corporations. This scale of operations means that Pricemart can achieve better economies of scale – especially in terms of the volume of products it sells – that can then be passed onto the customer in the form of a lower price, making it difficult for the competition to beat.

In addition to offering the widest array of products among top retailers (including its own private-label brand), Pricemart has long been touted for its technologically advanced information system for order tracking, inventory, customer relationship management and sales that can be tracked in real time for better decision-making and more efficient supply-chain management, enabling its stores to ensure that shelves are stocked at all times in order to maximize sales. However, the company has notoriously high employee turnover – largely due to a lack of social responsibility towards its workers, which also reflects poorly on the company’s image and makes for a poor customer experience. Moreover, there is a no-frills, warehouse-like experience in shopping at Pricemart, with little ambience and crowded checkout lines that add to the friction of in-store shopping. Moreover, a number of Pricemart’s low-cost products are of inferior quality, further diminishing the brand’s overall image.

AIM

Cheap-but-chic Aim is the nation’s No. 2 big-box retail chain (behind Pricemart). The fashion-forward discounter operates stores across the U.S., as well as Aim.com, which offers free in-store pickup as a part of a recent effort to create a more frictionless shopping experience. The company has also launched a perk-fueled loyalty program through its new AIMcard – a credit or debit card that provides discounts for in-store purchases, along with special deals aimed at “improving the guest experience,” and guarantees free two-day shipping (primarily as a way to compete with Cadabra).

With its dedicated CRM department and polished “discount store” reputation that positions it as a more posh version of Pricemart, Aim has been able to achieve a high level of customer satisfaction and good brand equity while maintaining relatively low-priced goods. Like most of its retail compatriots, the company is also focused on improving digital sales and providing customers with a seamless experience whether they are shopping in stores, online, or through its mobile app. But despite investing billions into high-tech enhancements in recent years, physical stores are still of huge importance to Aim’s growth strategy, including store redesigns and the opening of small-format urban stores. However, the retailer’s aggressive push to keep up with Cadabra and Pricemart, both online and in grocery sales, has led to significantly shrinking margins. And, even though Aim is spending above the industry average on shopping experience, it still has not been able to compete with the leading industry players in terms of innovation.

KTBENNEY

KTBenney Company, Inc. is one of the nation’s largest apparel and home retailers, with an expansive footprint of over a thousand locations throughout the U.S. – mostly in suburban shopping malls – as well as ktb.com. With its diverse mix of in-house, private label, and popular, mid-tier brands, KTBenney has been able to maintain a fairly strong omni-channel presence despite its status as a low-end mall anchor hampered by ever-declining foot traffic.

But even with a century-long legacy of solid brand equity, the aging department-store chain has struggled to stay relevant amid a changing retail landscape and had to shutter stores in recent years. To counter its weak apparel sales, KTBenney has been increasingly investing in its e-commerce platform, and – given that the majority of its retail locations will remain stuck in malls – the company is currently banking on the success of its new “store-in-stores” concept for high-end brands with the hope of winning back mall shoppers who may be attracted to the specially branded in-location buying experience.

ZEPHARA

Zephara is a Paris–based French multinational chain of clothing stores, featuring hundreds of brands, along with its own private label. The company achieved sustained growth across all areas of operation last year, and with omni-channel at the heart of its latest strategy, online sales have advanced rapidly. That said, the in-store shopping experience is what Zephara is most known for and, increasingly, it has integrated a variety of digital tools to optimize client experiences before, during and after their store visit. Recent enhancements to its mobile Virtual Assistant app points to the company’s commitment to use technology combined with personal service as a key to its future.

Zephara initially went after the prestige segment, drawing higher-end customers to their stores with top fashion brands previously only carried in select department stores, building its brand on younger, urban affluents. But in recent years it has partnered with more mass-market, lower-priced clothing brands and developed its own private label as a low-cost alternative to the name brands. This has been part of a larger effort to appeal to older, more cost-conscious customers while still maintaining its luxury look and feel in the store environment.

At Zephara, the sales floor is called the “stage,” and everywhere else is “backstage.” Sales associates are “cast members,” and managers are the “directors” – and the red-trimmed black dress the cast members wear is a “costume” (not a uniform). For this company, personalizing retail is all about the “offline experience” and it drives value from physical retail beyond sales per square foot, using its personable sales “cast” and in-store technologies to engage clients by solving for specific consumer pain points – like leveraging augmented reality to let customers “virtually” try on clothes, body scanning booths for better size fitting, and more. However, the company knows that customer loyalty is essential to its survival, and as e-commerce apparel sales rise on sites like Cadabra, that loyalty becomes more elusive, creating new challenges for a business based on foot traffic.

BESTCO

Bestco is a global retailer that operates a chain of membership-only warehouse clubs. As of today, Bestco is the second largest retailer in the world after Pricemart.

The operating philosophy is simple: Keep costs down and pass the savings on to members. Bestco’s large membership base and tremendous buying power, combined with a quest for efficiency, has resulted in its reputation for offering the best possible prices of bulk retail purchases for its members. It can consistently offer the lowest prices due to the efficient management of inventory, keeping overhead costs down, and generally reducing transaction costs at every step. While offering far fewer brands than other retailers, Bestco warehouses contain products carefully chosen based on quality, price, brand, and features to offer the best overall value.

With its low margins, high volume, and steady membership renewal rates, Bestco does not need to elevate the in-store experience – yet. The company’s austere warehouse layout, long checkout lines, and security checks at the exit are largely made up for by customers’ desire for savings. But with more online businesses cropping up that specialize in direct-to-consumer goods fulfillment, Bestco faces the eventual risk of being washed out in a future wave of budget-stretching options for consumers that come right to their door – options that would also better align with the retail economics of doing business within the digital age.
