Executive Summary
At the start of the simulation, Getchells was a legacy department store with a name customers knew and a business that was slipping. It trailed in Frictionless, Experience, and Low Price, posted a $6.3 million net loss, and carried 90 days of inventory.
To turn Getchells around, we eventually committed to winning in Brand, an existing strength, and competing with Zephara in Experience, which at least half of the segments valued, while aiming for at least fair value everywhere else. As Getchells’ leadership team, we arrived at this strategy through debate, stalemates, votes, and several corrections over eight rounds. The course frameworks (STP, the 3 C’s, the 4 P’s, and the Retail Success Matrix) structured our decisions. 
By Round 8, Getchells ranked first in Brand (6.0) and tied Zephara for first in Experience (6.6), up from 5.8 in Round 0. We cleared fair value in Low Price (4.7 vs. 3.7) but fell short in Frictionless (3.9 vs. 4.6). Customer lifetime value grew about 2.3 times. Stronger brands let us raise markups to 68% while gross margin climbed from 36.2% to 40.5%, and a leading experience gave customers reasons to return. Market value grew more than elevenfold, from $93.2 million to $1.038 billion.
1. Goals and Metrics
Question 1: We are evaluating teams on "Market Value," however, you may be using other metrics as well to measure your progress. What were your goals for the game, and which other metrics were you prioritizing and why?
Maximizing market value was a primary objective, but not the only one. In class, we learned that marketing, operations, and finance share one purpose, creating and capturing customer value, because superior value drives loyalty and profits. So we built our strategy on what customers valued, using STP to choose who we serve and where to compete.
Starting with the 3Cs. Understanding the company, customers, and competitors shaped our approach to STP. Our collaborators, especially our vendors, supplied the brands we carried and helped keep our costs competitive. We did not aim to lead on price, but with competitors clustered so close together, we could not afford to fall behind. From that analysis, we set four goals.
Choose a customer and win them (STP). We tracked segment preferences, market size, and revenue by segment to check that we were reaching our chosen customers.
Win Brand, contend in Experience, hold the rest. We aimed to lead Brand and close the gap with Zephara in Experience. Low Price and Frictionless were threshold metrics, where we aimed to stay within reach of fair value.
Spend where it moves customers. Each lever had to earn its budget. We watched whether spending and pricing actually moved the quadrant score targeted and benchmarked against industry averages and maximums.
Keep the business healthy. We tracked gross margin, net income, inventory days, financing costs, and WACC. Gross margin rose from 36.2% to 40.5%, net income went from a $6.3 million loss to $75.8 million, and inventory days fell from 90 to 73. Interest expense started at $30.1 million and WACC rose from 7.8% to 10.0%, yet market value still grew on stronger profits and cash flows.
We tracked all four goals together because the simulation calculates market value from projected free cash flow and WACC. A marketing investment still had to justify its cost, and a cost cut could hurt the brand, which we learned when our Round 1 depth likely contributed to Brand falling from 6.3 to 3.7. It was a lesson we carried forward, as we increased our depth in later rounds.
2. Choosing Our Target Segment
Question 2: The key to strategy (as discussed in class) is choosing your STP decision. The game provides information about the various segments in this market and their preferences, segment size (and growth can be assessed over time). How did you choose your target segment(s)? Discuss why your team chose that segment(s). It is useful to back up these choices with data in terms of the 3 C’s (customer, competition and company).
We chose Segment 3, the Brand and Experience shoppers, as our primary target and kept Segment 6 as a secondary focus. We did not start there. Early on we considered Segments 1, 2, 5, and 6, believing we could overtake Zephara in Experience, take the lead in Low Price because rivals were clustered, and compete in Frictionless. After we submitted Round 1, we realized it would not be that simple. Spreading across four segments made it impossible to prioritize spending, so we went back to the 3 C’s and STP, asking ourselves: what are we good at, who can we serve, and can we serve them profitably?
Customer. Segment 3 weights shopping experience at 0.4 and brand at 0.3, the strongest fit with what Getchells could offer. Because these shoppers cared more about brand than price, we assumed our higher-cost product decisions, especially which brands we carried and at what quality, would matter most to them. Assessed on the attractiveness-fit grid, Segment 3 was high fit but lower attractiveness because of its size, which made domination essential, so we committed to winning Brand. To grow, we also needed a more attractive segment with enough fit. Segment 6 filled that role.  Segment 6 grew from 17.9% to 19.5%, supporting the slight slip in Segment 3’s share of CLV from 11.9% to 11.1% by Round 8. Together, the two segments gave us a defensible position and room to grow, without giving up the Brand edge that set us apart.
Competition. Low Price was crowded. PriceMart and AIM aligned to Segment 2, BestCo dominated Segment 6’s value shopper, and our rivals were clustered on price. Brand was the opposite, as we already led by a clear margin (6.3 vs. Zephara’s 4.9 in Round 0). 
Company. Getchells was one of the nation’s most recognizable names and an anchor in hundreds of malls, with long relationships with national brands and a product range from designer labels to bargain brands. It also faced challenges. Shoppers were moving away from indoor malls toward more convenient ways to buy, and Getchells was behind on the technology that convenience requires. Low inventory turnover had also worn down the in-store experience it was once known for. Those weaknesses showed us where we could not win outright and where we simply had to keep up.
Putting the 3 C’s together, winning Segment 3 meant dominating in Brand, but growth would have to come from Experience, where our gap to Zephara was small enough to close (5.8 vs. 6.5). The team put the pairing to a vote and Brand with Experience won  4–2. By the end, revenue had grown in absolute terms in the segments that valued Experience. More surprisingly, revenue also grew in those that valued Low Price. Remaining competitive on price, which was helped by our vendor management investments, contributed to growth.
3. Creating Value for Our Target
Question 3: Once the strategy was chosen, your team needed to create value for these segments vis-à-vis the tools of the game available to you. What were your priorities here?
To create value for our target, Segment 3, we combined the
Retail Success Matrix, targeting, and positioning from class and the four eras of marketing. We leaned mostly on the customer and experience era, borrowed enough from enabling technology to avoid falling behind, and applied production-era discipline to stay credible on price. Our target was not especially price-sensitive, so we expected quality and a stronger experience to raise willingness to pay.
Marketing and customer (Brand). Quality became our clearest point of difference, so we raised private-brand quality to 10/10 and held it there so store labels would help the brand rather than dilute it. We rebuilt depth after the Round 1 cut. Depth ties up working capital, but it also builds customer loyalty, and the cut had saved money at the expense of the value we were creating for customers. We also tuned the private-brand mix by price tiers. Private brands made up 40% of higher-cost products, where premium shoppers want national and designer names, and 65% of lower-cost products, where store brands support margin and price.
Experience (place and service). We wanted more control over the customer experience, so we funded the levers that touch the shopper’s experience directly. By the final round we spent about $29 million on customer service, $21 million on shopping experience, and $16.94 million on customer analytics. We also finished with 250 stores, up from 200, to support the shopping experience we were building.
Production (Low Price). We invested heavily in vendor management early to keep supplier relationships strong. We priced for product quality and brand value rather than be the cheapest, raising markups gradually to 68%. That risked some price-sensitive shoppers, but Low Price still finished above fair value (4.7 vs. 3.7).
Enabling technology (Frictionless). We invested in online early on, but then realized, using industry benchmarks, that we would not beat Cadabra and needed to be more targeted. We still wanted customers to have access to online shopping, so we held our online investment at about $10 million and tested whether service and analytics could lift Frictionless alongside it. They helped, but not enough. Frictionless finished below fair value (3.9 vs. 4.6).
4. How Our Strategy Changed
Question 4: Did your team strategy change over time? Why or why not? How did you use the Retail Success Matrix in planning your short-term and long-term strategy? How did you consider your investments (and debt) from a short-term and long-term perspective?
Our biggest strategy shift came early, as we learned the levers. In Rounds 1 and 2, our main focus was repairing Getchells’ finances, in parallel with the marketing strategy. Investing most of our capital in vendor management and cutting costs, including product depth. That helped return us to positive net income, but the depth cut likely contributed to our initial Brand score drop from 6.3 to 3.7. ….Early on we considered Segments 1, 2, 5, and 6, believing we could overtake Zephara in Experience, take the lead in Low Price because rivals were clustered, and compete in Frictionless. After we submitted Round 1, we realized it would not be that simple. Spreading across four segments made it impossible to prioritize spending, so we went back to the 3 C’s and STP, asking ourselves: what are we good at, who can we serve, and can we serve them profitably?
By Rounds 3 and 4, we had turned to the customer while still watching our finances, choosing Experience as Brand’s partner and naming Segment 3 as our target.
In Rounds 5 through 8, we leaned fully into the strategy and hedged less. The clearest example was vendor management, which we cut by 60% from its peak, moving that money into service, analytics, and shopping experience. Experience rose, while our vendor management score held near 100% and Low Price stayed above fair value. 
Using the Matrix. Each round we checked where we stood against the fair value line, where competitors were clustered, and whether a quadrant was one to win or one to hold. The clustering on Low Price led to a decision to stay out of the fight for first. The Matrix also flagged drift, such as our Experience decline before we found the right mix. We paired the Matrix with the Product Position chart, tracking where Zephara invested in shopping experience levers, moving our spending in the same direction. 
Short-term vs. long-term spending. Customer service and analytics moved scores quickly. Stores and online investment were long-term and changed debt, WACC, and future cash flow. Vendor management behaved like a long-term investment that kept paying off, since the supplier strength we built early held after we cut spending. We also looked for levers where the same dollar could do double duty. Customer analytics was the clearest example, feeding both Experience and Frictionless. 
Stores and debt. Stores were our hardest financial call. An early increase pushed WACC up. Unsure whether selling stores would hurt Brand and Experience, we waited, reduced gradually, and once our financial position improved, we expanded again and finished at 250. More stores supported the experience we were building, but WACC rose from 7.8% in Round 0 to 10% in Round 8. By the end, this was not as concerning given how much the health of our company had improved. 
5. Allocating Time and Dollars
Question 5: Resources in the game are constrained: how did you decide how to allocate your time/$’s?
Other than choosing segments, deciding where to spend was our biggest debate. Every dollar had to pass four tests:
Strategic fit. Does it support Brand, Experience, or a threshold quadrant?
Marginal impact. Is more spending still moving the score? 
Benchmarks. Where do we sit against the industry average and maximum? 
Threshold logic. Is this quadrant one to win or one to hold within reach of fair value? We came to realize that holding a quadrant, as we did with Low Price, cost far less than winning it.
Time was the other constraint. We spent at least 10 hours together, and early meetings ran long until each member brought proposed numbers in advance. We focused on disagreements and voted when needed, so each vote was an informed one.

Part II. Overall Learnings from the Game and Team Interactions
Question 1: How did your team organize itself to develop a "plan of attack"?
After a first meeting spent debating nearly every number, we changed the process. Each member came with proposed numbers and the logic behind them, which we compared in a shared spreadsheet so we could focus on the disagreements. Roles formed around interest, with some of us on finance and WACC, others on segments and positioning, and others on the levers and competitor benchmarks. By the final rounds, we started with our priorities, hypothesized how each change would affect results, entered the numbers, and adjusted our lever allocations as needed.
Question 2: Were there any decisions that your team had difficulty reaching a consensus on? If yes, what were the critical issues that were discussed and how did you find a resolution?
Our biggest debate was how to grow when Brand mainly reached Segment 3, which held one of the smallest shares of market CLV. The choice came down to pairing Brand with Experience or forgoing our incumbent leadership in Brand to capture the perceived growth potential from larger customer segments that valued Experience and Low Price. One side argued for Low Price, since we were close to the leaders, and several larger segments cared about price. The other side argued that rivals were clustered on price, while Experience mattered to Segment 3, at least half of the segments, and put us within striking distance of Zephara. Brand and Experience won the vote. Stores were a recurring debate. Some of us wanted fewer stores to protect WACC, while others worried that closing stores would weaken the Brand and Experience we were building, so we tested both directions over the simulation. The private-brand mix split us too, weighing margin against premium shoppers’ preference for natural names. In each case, whoever disagreed had to make their case with numbers, and when we still could not agree, we voted.
Question 3: Which of the data reports that you received did your team find especially useful? Which ones did your team ignore? What were the insights that you got from figuring out what kinds of data you found yourself prioritizing?
The Retail Success Matrix showed where we stood against competitors and fair value. Segment preference and CLV data shaped our targeting. Industry benchmarks told us when we were underspending, and financial reports told us whether marketing gains were turning into profit. We paid less attention to the vendor metric once it held near 100%, and to reports that did not connect to a decision. The pricing chart and the market share split between higher- and lower-cost products were the hardest to read. Each showed a single outcome that many levers could have moved, so they told us what changed but rarely why.
Question 4: What were your chief "takeaways" from playing this game? What insights did it give you about determining "go to market" strategies in general and implementing those strategies? What insights from playing this game do you think you might use in real life?
The biggest lesson was that strategy means choosing where not to win and we fell below fair value in Frictionless as the cost of funding Brand and Experience. STP was harder in practice than in theory, since our small target meant growth had to come from neighboring segments. We saw firsthand what we learned in class about creating and capturing customer value. No decision sat in a silo. Marketing, operations, and finance had to be balanced together, since saving money in one area could erode the value customers see, and every marketing investment still had to earn its cost. Our mistakes taught us as much as our successes. Spreading across four segments early, cutting depth in Round 1, and working to hold fair value online before we reallocated capital, all cost us time and money in the simulation. In our own work, we expect to define the target before choosing tactics, separate the metrics we must win from those we only need to hold, ask whether each dollar still moves results, and use data as a tool to settle indecision.
